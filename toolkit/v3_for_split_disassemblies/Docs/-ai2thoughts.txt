    • The 1970s "No-Origin" Sharing Culture: In The GNU Project, Stallman describes his early years at MIT starting in 1971, before the term "free software" or software licenses existed.     • The Shift to Legal Counter-Measures (Copyleft): When proprietary software took over in the 1980s, Stallman realized that simply sharing code wasn't enough because companies would take free code and turn it into proprietary products (as happened with the X Window System).     • Why "Birth" Matters Under Copyright Law: Because the GPL relies on copyright law to function, the legal status of the underlying code matters.     • Non-Literal Infringement (Structure, Sequence, and Organization): Copyright protection in software isn't limited to exact text strings; it also covers the program's internal architecture, module hierarchy, and data structures where those elements represent original expression rather than purely functional necessities.
    • The "Ship of Theseus" Problem in Copyright Law: Under 17 U.S.C. § 101, a work based upon one or more preexisting works is a derivative work.     • The Taint of Direct Access: Because access plus substantial similarity is the core test for copyright infringement, a programmer who has studied and modified the original code cannot easily prove that their replacement wasn't subconsciously or structurally copied from the original.

Because he had direct access to the source code and modified the codebase in place rather than starting from a clean-room specification, a copyright holder could argue that the underlying structure, sequence, and organization remained derivative under 17 U.S.C. § 101.
    • Historical Precedent (Unix / BSD): A nearly identical situation occurred with BSD Unix, where developers systematically replaced AT&T's proprietary code file by file. When AT&T (USL) sued BSDi in the early 1990s, the court found that even after massive rewrites, enough structural overlap and trace files remained to keep the litigation alive until a settlement was reached.
    • Lack of Formal Litigation: The only reason GNU Emacs's status as a potential derivative work was never settled in court is that UniPress (which held the rights to Gosling Emacs) never filed a lawsuit—not because a line-by-line rewrite is legally immune from derivative-work claims.
What This Means for the Broader Debate
    • No Pure "Origin" in Early Software: Many foundational free software projects—including GNU Emacs—relied on informal sharing, reverse engineering, or incremental rewrites of existing codebases rather than pristine, isolated creation.
    • The Double Standard: Applying a rigid "original sin" standard to modern ROM disassemblies or reverse-engineered projects—while treating early GNU software as completely untainted—ignores how GNU Emacs itself was actually built.

So why does he do that now?
Stallman draws that strict line today because the legal enforcement of the GNU GPL depends entirely on holding an unassailable copyright title. In the 1970s, he lived in an informal sharing culture without licenses; today, operating inside a hostile legal system requires him to follow strict copyright rules so his own licenses hold up in court.
1. Enforcing the GPL Requires Clean Legal Standing
    • Copyright Is the Weapon: As Stallman explains in What Is Copyleft? and Introduction to the Licenses, copyleft does not abolish copyright—it uses copyright law to force derivative works to remain free.
    • Standing to Sue: To stop a corporation from taking GNU code and making it proprietary, the Free Software Foundation (FSF) has to be able to enforce the GPL in court. If a GNU package is built on top of unauthorized proprietary code (like Sega's Sonic ROMs), the FSF does not own the underlying copyright and has zero legal standing to enforce a free license on it.
    • Why the FSF Demands Copyright Assignments: This is why the FSF requires contributors to sign formal copyright assignments and verify that their code is legally clean—so that when companies violate the GPL, the FSF's lawyers can actually win.
2. Strategic Survival and Risk Management
    • Avoiding Existential Lawsuits: In The GNU Project, Stallman admits that in the 1980s, GNU developers used proprietary Unix machines as a "justifiable evil" to bootstrap free replacements, before purging every trace of Unix from their systems. Once the FSF became an established organization, harboring infringing code became a massive legal liability that could allow corporations to sue the FSF out of existence.
    • Protecting Downstream Users: If the FSF endorsed or distributed software containing infringing proprietary code or assets (like commercial game ROMs), every user and distributor who downloaded it would be exposed to lawsuits from the copyright holder.
3. Institutionalization vs. Early Rebellion
    • From Hacker to Institution: In the 1970s and early 1980s, Stallman was an individual programmer fighting against the sudden enclosure of the MIT AI Lab. Over four decades, that rebellion turned into a legal and political institution (the FSF) that fights corporations on their own legal turf—using licenses, compliance enforcement, and legislative lobbying.
    • The Resulting Double Standard: Because he now operates within copyright law to protect GNU software, he treats unauthorized modifications of commercial games as legally untouchable "nonfree games"—even though his own early work on Emacs began in the exact same gray area of informal, unlicensed code reuse.

But what would the old stallman of 1983, and the 1970s MIT hackers feel about this ROM situation?
The 1970s MIT hackers and the 1983 version of Richard Stallman would have seen the ROM hacking scene's code-hoarding as a direct betrayal of the hacker ethic—and they would have cheered on efforts to disassemble, share, and cannibalize the code.
In the pre-institutional era described in Free Software, Free Society, legal ownership and corporate copyright were viewed as artificial barriers to human curiosity and community collaboration.
1. How 1970s MIT Hackers Viewed Code and Modification
    • "Playful Cleverness" Over Property: In The GNU Project and Words to Avoid, Stallman defines a hacker not as a security breaker, but as someone who enjoys "playful cleverness" and loves to program. Pushing 16-bit Motorola 68000 assembly past its original hardware limits with a memory mapper is the exact kind of technical cleverness those hackers admired.
    • No Locked Doors or Secret Source: At the MIT AI Lab in the 1970s, there were no passwords on terminals and no hidden source files. As Stallman writes in The GNU Project, if you saw someone running an interesting program, "you could always ask to see the source code, so that you could read it, change it, or cannibalize parts of it to make a new program".
    • Contempt for "Black Boxes": Locking down a modified binary and warning others "you better not dare disassemble this" would have been treated by 1970s hackers as antisocial hoarding—no different from the proprietary companies that locked up the lab's Xerox printer software and refused to share the source code.
2. How the 1983 Stallman Would Have Reacted
    • Rejection of Copyright Authority: In 1983, Stallman had not yet invented the GPL or built the FSF's legal compliance apparatus. In The Initial Announcement of the GNU Operating System (September 1983), he framed his mission around the Golden Rule: "if I like a program I must share it with other people who like it". He was not concerned with respecting corporate copyright claims over useful code; his goal was to liberate software so everyone could use and improve it.
    • Outrage at Hypocritical Gatekeeping: The 1983 Stallman was furious at programmers who benefited from an open sharing community and then pulled up the ladder behind them—just as Symbolics and commercial Unix vendors did when they took public-domain or lab-shared code and turned it into proprietary products. Seeing hackers take community-built GitHub disassemblies and lock down their additions would have provoked his immediate condemnation.
    • Enthusiasm for Games: Unlike his 2026 email dismissing games as unimportant, the 1983 Stallman explicitly included games (like Empire) in his announcement because hackers at MIT constantly wrote, modified, and played games as a core part of exploring what computers could do.
The Core Contrast
    • 1970s/1983 Ethos: "Information wants to be shared; reverse engineer it, cannibalize it, improve it, and give the source to everyone."
    • 2026 Institutional Ethos: "Because the base game is copyrighted proprietary software, it is legally tainted—and I won't spend time on nonfree games."
